**Generation of continuous environmental predictor surfaces**
   
   1. Assess monitoring-station spatial coverage prior to interpolation.
      - Map station distributions for each environmental parameter.
      - Identify spatial clustering.
      - Identify portions of PBS lacking observations.
      - Exclude variables where development of a continuous surface would consist primarily of spatial extrapolation.
   
   2. Account for coastal barriers and complex estuarine geometry.
      - Straight-line Euclidean distance can incorrectly represent spatial relationships between monitoring locations separated by peninsulas, islands, and other land barriers [@Stachelek2015].
      - Barrier-aware interpolation will therefore be evaluated for the PBS environmental surfaces [@Stachelek2015].
   
   3. Evaluate inverse path distance weighting (IPDW) as a candidate interpolation method.
      - IPDW uses aquatic-path distance rather than straight-line Euclidean distance when determining spatial relationships among observations [@Stachelek2015].
      - This approach was developed for coastal water-quality interpolation where land barriers influence spatial connectivity [@Stachelek2015].
   
   4. Compare candidate interpolation approaches.
      - Evaluate inverse path distance weighting (IPDW) [@Stachelek2015].
      - Evaluate conventional inverse distance weighting (IDW).
      - Evaluate ordinary kriging.
      - Use spatially withheld monitoring locations to evaluate predictive performance and reduce bias associated with spatial dependence [@Roberts2017; @Valavi2019].
      - Compare root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and prediction bias among interpolation methods.
      - Select the final interpolation method based on predictive performance and ecological appropriateness.
   
   5. Produce final environmental predictor rasters.
      - Use a common projected coordinate reference system.
      - Use a common cell size.
      - Use a common raster origin and alignment.
      - Mask predictions to aquatic habitat.
      - Document the final raster resolution.
      - Maintain spatial compatibility with the existing PBS habitat-suitability framework where appropriate [@Geselbracht2024].

6. **Oyster occurrence preparation**
   
   1. Clip FWC oyster-bed features to the study area.
      - Remove oyster occurrences outside the PBS modeling extent [@FWC2026Oysters].
   
   2. Convert mapped oyster-bed polygons into representative MaxEnt presence observations.
      - Use a reproducible procedure that avoids disproportionately representing large or densely mapped oyster polygons [@Phillips2009; @Merow2013].
      - Document the initial number of oyster polygons and final number of presence observations.
   
   3. Evaluate spatial clustering of presence observations.
      - Apply spatial thinning where necessary to reduce clustering and sampling bias [@Phillips2009].
      - Base thinning decisions on spatial dependence and sampling structure rather than arbitrary data reduction [@Phillips2009; @Merow2013].

7. **Background sampling**
   
   1. Define the accessible/background modeling region.
      - Restrict background sampling to aquatic habitat within the Pensacola Bay System [@Merow2013].
      - Require complete environmental predictor coverage within the background domain.
      - Exclude terrestrial areas and locations outside the modeled environmental domain [@Phillips2009; @Merow2013].
   
   2. Generate MaxEnt background locations.
      - Document the final number of background points.
      - Sample background locations from the defined accessible region rather than from an arbitrary rectangular geographic extent [@Phillips2009; @Merow2013].
   
   3. Address occurrence-sampling bias.
      - Evaluate whether spatial thinning, bias weighting, or restricted background sampling is necessary [@Phillips2009].

8. **Predictor screening**
   
   1. Assemble the final candidate predictor stack.
      - Salinity [@LaPeyre2021; @Barnes2007; @Pollack2012; @Geselbracht2024].
      - Dissolved oxygen [@LaPeyre2021; @Pollack2012; @Geselbracht2024].

      - Substrate/bottom type, if suitable data are obtained [@LaPeyre2021; @Barnes2007; @Geselbracht2024].
   
   2. Evaluate correlation and redundancy among predictors.
      - Calculate pairwise correlations among candidate environmental variables [@Dormann2013].
      - Identify highly correlated or ecologically redundant predictors [@Dormann2013].
      - Retain predictors based on ecological relevance, data quality, and interpretability while limiting problematic collinearity [@Dormann2013].

9. **MaxEnt model fitting and tuning**
   
   1. Fit MaxEnt using oyster occurrence locations, background samples, and the finalized environmental predictor stack [@Phillips2006; @Elith2011].
   
   2. Tune model complexity rather than relying exclusively on default settings.
      - Evaluate multiple regularization multipliers [@Merow2013; @Kass2021].
      - Evaluate multiple feature-class combinations [@Merow2013; @Kass2021].
   
   3. Use the `ENMeval` framework for model tuning and evaluation [@Kass2021].
   
   4. Select the final MaxEnt configuration.
      - Consider predictive performance [@Kass2021].
      - Consider omission rates [@Kass2021].
      - Consider model complexity and overfitting [@Merow2013; @Kass2021].
   
   5. Generate the final continuous habitat-suitability surface.
      - Retain continuous predictions for the primary spatial comparison with the HSI [@Elith2011; @Merow2013].
   
   6. Evaluate environmental-variable influence.
      - Examine permutation importance [@Merow2013].
      - Examine model response curves [@Elith2011; @Merow2013].
      - Evaluate other supported measures of predictor contribution [@Merow2013].
      - Interpret these associations as predictive relationships rather than direct evidence of ecological causation [@Elith2011].

10. **Spatial model validation**
    
    1. Use spatial rather than purely random cross-validation.
       - Nearby oyster occurrences may experience highly similar environmental conditions, violating assumptions of independence [@Roberts2017].
       - Random training/test partitions may therefore produce overly optimistic estimates of predictive performance [@Roberts2017; @Valavi2019].
    
    2. Divide oyster occurrences into spatially separated validation folds.
       - Select spatial-block dimensions based on observed spatial structure rather than arbitrary distances [@Valavi2019].
    
    3. Evaluate candidate models against withheld spatial folds.
       - Report appropriate presence-background model-performance metrics [@Phillips2006; @Kass2021].
       - Evaluate omission rates and AUC where appropriate [@Phillips2006; @Kass2021].
       - Report variation in model performance among spatial folds [@Valavi2019; @Kass2021].

11. **Comparison with the existing Habitat Suitability Index**
    
    1. Harmonize the MaxEnt and HSI raster surfaces.
       - Match geographic extent to permit spatial comparison [@Johnson2005; @Geselbracht2024].
       - Match coordinate reference systems.
       - Match cell size and raster alignment.
       - Ensure suitability scores are represented on directly comparable scales where possible [@Johnson2005].
    
    2. Compare continuous habitat-suitability predictions.
       - Calculate cell-by-cell correlation between HSI and MaxEnt predictions [@Johnson2005].
       - Consider Pearson and/or Spearman correlation depending on the characteristics of the model-output distributions [@Johnson2005].
    
    3. Generate a spatial difference surface [@Johnson2005].
       
       $$
       D_i = S_{MaxEnt,i} - S_{HSI,i}
       $$
       
       - Values near zero represent relatively strong agreement between the two suitability estimates.
       - Positive values identify locations where MaxEnt predicts relatively greater suitability.
       - Negative values identify locations where the HSI predicts relatively greater suitability.
    
    4. Evaluate categorical agreement if suitability classes are created.
       - Identify locations where both models predict high suitability [@Johnson2005].
       - Identify locations where both models predict low suitability [@Johnson2005].
       - Identify locations where MaxEnt predicts high suitability while the HSI predicts low suitability [@Johnson2005].
       - Identify locations where the HSI predicts high suitability while MaxEnt predicts low suitability [@Johnson2005].
       - Consider an agreement statistic such as Kappa if categorical suitability classes are used [@Johnson2005].
    
    5. Interpret disagreement between models.
       - Determine whether disagreement is geographically concentrated [@Johnson2005].
       - Evaluate whether disagreement corresponds with particular environmental predictors.
       - Consider differences in data availability and modeling assumptions between MaxEnt and the HSI [@Johnson2005; @Geselbracht2024].
       - Avoid treating either modeling approach as automatically representing ground truth [@Johnson2005].

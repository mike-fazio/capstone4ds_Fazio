\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

The eastern oyster (\emph{Crassostrea virginica}) is a foundational
species that provides numerous ecosystem goods and services such as food
and habitat production, shoreline stabilization and protection, water
quality improvement, nutrient cycling, and carbon sequestration
\citep{Geselbracht2024, Grabowski2012, Barnes2007}. Additionally,
eastern oysters provide numerous economic benefits such as supporting
oyster fisheries, recreational harvesting, tourism, along with the
associated employment that goes along with those industries
\citep{Geselbracht2024, LaPeyre2021}. In one study,
\citet{Grabowski2012} estimated the average annual value to range from
\$10,325 to \$99,421 per hectare (2011 Dollars).

In recent decades, oyster reefs have experienced substantial declines,
with only an estimated 15\% remaining worldwide, and 50 to 80\% being
lost within the Gulf region alone \citep{Pollack2012, LaPeyre2021}. The
decline is largely attributed to a combination of anthropogenic
activities such as overharvesting, habitat alteration, sedimentation and
water quality decline, disease and changing hydrology
\citep{LaPeyre2021, Geselbracht2024}. In response to these sharp
declines, many restoration efforts have been undertaken with varying
strategies and success rates; recently, planning documents released by
the Deepwater Horizon Natural Resource Damage Assessment Trustees called
for large-scale development of reef networks to increase resilience
\citep{Geselbracht2024}.

\subsection{Oyster decline in the Pensacola Bay
System}\label{oyster-decline-in-the-pensacola-bay-system}

One such system to experience oyster decline is the Pensacola Bay System
(PBS). The PBS is a large estuarine system located in Escambia and Santa
Rosa counties in northwest Florida. The system is comprised of multiple
water bodies including Pensacola Bay, Escambia Bay, Blackwater Bay, East
Bay, and Santa Rosa Sound; and is influenced heavily by freshwater input
from the Escambia, Blackwater and Yellow Rivers \citep{Christensen1997}.
Historically, the PBS supported extensive oyster reefs that provided
critical habitat for a variety of species and substantial economic value
to the surrounding communities \citep{Christensen1997}. However, over
the past several decades, the extent of oyster habitat within the PBS
has declined significantly due to a combination of factors including
overharvesting, habitat degradation, and water quality issues. One
estimate puts the decline in oyster habitat from 15,000 ha in 1883 to
91.5 ha in 2021 \citep{Geselbracht2024}. This decline has prompted
efforts to restore oyster reefs within the PBS
\citep{PPBEP_OysterRestoration, TNC2021OysterRestoration}, with multiple
studies aiming to identify the most suitable areas within the estuary to
focus restoration efforts
\citep{Geselbracht2024, Christensen1997, PPBEP_OysterRestoration}.

With limited resources available for restoration, it is critical to
identify areas where chances of oyster survival and long-term reef
establishment are at their highest, and where poor site selection could
ultimately lead to unsatisfactory results. As with any ecosystem, the
environmental conditions within the PBS vary greatly in time and space,
and such variations can have great impacts on oyster survival and
establishment. Therefore, it is important to understand the
environmental factors that drive oyster occurrence so that scientists
and policymakers can make informed decisions regarding said restoration
site selection \citep{Pollack2012, Barnes2007, Geselbracht2024}.

\subsection{Environmental controls on Eastern oyster
habitat}\label{environmental-controls-on-eastern-oyster-habitat}

Oyster recruitment, survival, and reef establishment are influenced by
complex interactions between numerous physical, chemical, and biological
environmental factors. As such, determining the most influential factors
is a multivariate problem that requires careful consideration of each
factor and its interactions with others
\citep{LaPeyre2021, Pollack2012, Barnes2007, Christensen1997}. To help
narrow the focus, the most commonly cited environmental factors were
examined in the literature and are summarized here.

Perhaps the most influential and widely reported environmental driver of
oyster suitability is salinity, which represents an important
environmental gradient within estuarine systems that influences
physiology, recruitment, disease, predation, and survival. Other
important characteristics often reported are shown in
Table~\ref{tbl-environmental-drivers} and include dissolved oxygen,
temperature, depth, turbidity, and bottom type (substrate)
\citep{LaPeyre2021, Pollack2012, Barnes2007, Geselbracht2024, Christensen1997}.

\subsection{Species Distribution
Modelling}\label{species-distribution-modelling}

Quantifying the relationships between the distribution of species and
the species-environment interactions has a long history in ecological
research, having been described as far back as the late 17th and early
18th centuries in which researchers observed biological patterns in
terms of their relationships with geographical and/or environmental
gradients \citep{Rushton2004, Elith2009}. Since then, the propagation of
large-scale spatial datasets, the development and integration of GIS,
increased access to statistical packages and computational power have
allowed for much more development in modeling complex
species-environment interactions \citep{Johnson2005}. Out of such
developments has arisen a technique known as Species Distribution
Modeling (SDM), which can be loosely defined as numerical tools that
combine observations of species occurrence or abundance with
environmental estimates \citep{Elith2009}. According to
\citet{Elith2009}, SDMs can be largely classified within several
families of models: expert-driven or heuristic, environmental-envelope,
environmental-similarity, regression-based, Bayesian and hierarchical,
machine-learning and data mining, and ensemble or multimodal.
Additionally, the selection of an SDM approach largely depends on a
multitude of factors such as: the study's objective, quality and
quantity of available data, ecological characteristics of the species,
spatial and temporal scale, whether input data includes absence data or
presence-only data \citep{Elith2009}. With so many choices of modeling
technique and the driving factors behind that choice, deciding on one
model can be quite difficult and the choice usually defaults to a
standard technique depending on the subject matter.

\emph{Habitat Suitability Indices}

One popular approach used in many studies involving the Eastern oyster
throughout the gulf region is that of the expert-driven Habitat
Suitability Index
\citep{Barnes2007, Pollack2012, Geselbracht2024, Christensen1997}. HSIs
are typically used in situations where quantitative data on species
distributions is difficult to collect or non-existent, relying on
expert-driven variable selection, meta-analysis, and classification
methods that transform environmental characteristics into standardized
estimates of habitat suitability
\citep{Barnes2007, Pollack2012, Geselbracht2024, Johnson2005}.

Within the PBS, HSIs have been used to develop GIS-based and
estuary-specific HSMs that integrate biological, physical, chemical, and
management-related variables in order to identify suitable restoration
areas with the system. In the mid 1990's \citet{Christensen1997}
developed an HSI for the eastern oyster in the PBS to demonstrate the
model framework's usefulness in illustrating species suitability in a
system wide approach. \citet{Geselbracht2024} then followed and further
expanded on the approach incorporating management factors into the
model, adding a layer of regulatory influence to the model output versus
the purely environmental analysis of the prior. Additionally, the
Pensacola and Perdido Bay Estuary Program recently built on-top of
\citet{Geselbracht2024} by incorporating some of their own data and
refining some of the weighting \citep{PPBEP_OysterRestoration}.

These previous studies alone highlight the strengths and weaknesses of
the HSI approaches. Some of the strengths include interoperability,
adaptability, and the integration of ecological knowledge, but the
results entirely rely on expert decisions regarding variable selection,
suitability functions, weighting and scoring, and how to aggregate the
results. All of these present opportunity for differing outputs even if
the same inputs were used by different groups
\citep{Geselbracht2024, Johnson2005}.

\emph{Maximum Entropy Modelling (MaxEnt)}

MaxEnt is a presence-background SDM framework based on general-purpose
machine learning methods that are useful for making predictions using
incomplete information by relating environmental conditions at known
occurrence locations to the environmental conditions throughout the
study area \citep{Phillips2006, Elith2011}. Some of the main benefits of
MaxEnt is that it uses presence-only data, reducing the need for the
often unavailable absence data, it has the ability to model complex
interactions between environmental conditions, and it has demonstrated
high predictive performance while limiting overfitting through
regularization \citep{Phillips2009, Merow2013, Elith2011}. An added
benefit to this particular study is the fact that MaxEnt is tailored
towards presence-only species occurrence data, making it highly suitable
for the data set used here in which the \citet{FWC2026Oysters} datasets
explicitly collected occurrence data without including absence
locations.

However, the use of such algorithms should not be made blindly as many
issues can arise if careful precautions are not taken. Due to the nature
of ecological many biases in the data samples can arise such as spatial
and temporal autocorrelation, group dependence structures, hierarchical
structures and multicollinearity leading to overfitting model parameters
and over-optimistic prediction confidence
\citep{Dormann2013, Kass2021, Roberts2017}. Thankfully, significant work
has been put into this through cross-validation techniques and the
development of model evaluation software packages such as ENMeval 2.0
implemented through the R statistical language
\citep{Kass2021, Roberts2017}

\subsection{Study Objective}\label{study-objective}

To demonstrate how a MaxEnt SDM can be used as a complementary tool to
help researchers and practitioners leading ecological restoration
activities, this study aims to develop an empirically fitted MaxEnt
habitat-suitability model using observed oyster occurrence data and
known environmental conditions. The primary objective will be to develop
a spatially explicit SDM representing relative Eastern oyster habitat
suitability throughout the PBS. Next, the resulting suitability surface
will be evaluated against the existing Pensacola Bay HSI developed by
\citet{Geselbracht2024} and iterated upon by
\citet{PPBEP_OysterRestoration}. To achieve this, environmental
predictors that are commonly associated with oyster habitat suitability
will be identified and obtained. The predictors will be used to fit the
MaxEnt model and model performance will be evaluated using spatially
separated cross-validation. Finally, we will compare the predicted
surface to the existing HSI to determine areas of agreement and
disagreement, and we will use these results to determine whether the
MaxEnt framework provides an empirical alternative or complement to
HSI-based restoration planning. It is hypothesized that the MaxEnt model
will successfully identify patterns of habitat suitability for the
eastern oyster within the PBS. Overall agreement between the HSI and
MaxEnt is expected to be high. However, MaxEnt is expected to produce a
smoother, more continuous suitability surface because of the empirically
fitted environmental relationships as opposed to predefined,
lower-resolution HSI suitability envelopes. As a result, the MaxEnt
model could prove beneficial to help narrow down project locations to
help reinforce expert-driven model outputs.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

All data analysis and processing was performed using the R programming
language \citep{r}. Environmental observation data was acquired from the
EPA's Water Quality Portal via the dataRetrieval r-package
\citep{wqp_2026, dataRetrieval2026}. Data QA/QC and preprocessing was
completed utilizing the Tidyverse package and any spatially aware data
processing was conducted using the package sf \citep{tidyverse, sf}. All
spatial data was projected to the NAD83(2011) / Florida North State
Plane Coordinate System (EPSG:6440) with distances in meters, which
minimizes spatial distortion in the study area.

\subsubsection{Study Area}\label{study-area}

The study area for this project is the Pensacola Bay System which
includes portions of Pensacola Bay, Escambia Bay, East Bay and
Blackwater Bay Figure~\ref{fig-study-area}. Boundaries of this study
area were achieved by accessing Florida Department of Environmental
Protection (FDEP) Waterbody IDs feature layer hosted on ArcGIS Online
\citep{FDEP2005WBIDs}. Once retrieved, the dataset was filtered to
include only relevant WBIDs (nine IDs in the 548 series) and then
combined to create a singular study area polygon. Creating the study
area polygon is an important first step as it was needed to extract
relevant environmental data from much larger datasets in the coming data
gathering. The study area was intentionally chosen in an attempt to
match the previous study area applied in \citet{Geselbracht2024}.

Oyster occurrence data was acquired from the Florida Fish and Wildlife

\citep{LaPeyre2021, Barnes2007, Pollack2012, Geselbracht2024}.

Bottom sediment data and oyster recruitment data was obtained from the
Pensacola and Perdido Bay Estuary Program although the data were first
reported by \citet{MacCauley2004} and \citet{Arnold2017}, respectively.

\subsubsection{Water-quality Data
Preprocessing}\label{water-quality-data-preprocessing}

All water quality parameters were inspected to ensure comparable
measurement units and analytical procedures and avoid combining
different forms of similar analytes. The IQR screen was applied to each
parameter using data from all months. Aggregation then used non-outlier
observations from the warm season (May through October). Outliers were
defined as follows. \[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Outlier if } \quad
x &< Q_{0.25} - 1.5(IQR) \\
\text{or} \quad
x &> Q_{0.75} + 1.5(IQR) \\
\
\text{where } IQR &= Q_{0.75} - Q_{0.25}
\end{aligned}
\]

Observations were averaged within station and year, then the annual
means were averaged within station. Station means were averaged within 2
km hexagonal bins clipped to the study area. Each bin was represented by
a point on its surface. Stations with one usable observation were
retained.

\subsubsection{Environmental Prediction
Rasters}\label{environmental-prediction-rasters}

Prior to use in the MaxEnt modeling framework, continuous prediction
rasters were created for dissolved oxygen, salinity, turbidity, and
water temperature using the Inverse Path Distance Weighting (IPDW)
package in R \citep{IPDW_2023}. IPDW is a variant of Inverse Distance
Weighting (IDW), which is a deterministic interpolation method that
allows for the estimation of point values using the equation:

\[ 
V= \frac{\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1} v_i {d^{-p}_{i}}}{\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1} {d^{-p}_{i}}} 
\]

where \(d\) is the distance between the prediction and measurement
points, \(v_i\) is the parameter value, and \(p\) is a power parameter.
However, in IPDW \(d\) is a calculated path-distance value rather than a
typical Euclidean distance as used in IDW \citep{Stachelek2015}.

IPDW was selected due to the model's ability to be barrier aware and
avoid interference or influence from crossing land boundaries that can
be introduced when using more traditional Euclidean based interpolation
methods \citep{Stachelek2015}. This is especially important given the
geography on PBS where the Garcon Point area is a narrow, low lying
peninsula separating Escambia Bay to the west and Blackwater Bay to the
east. Both of which are significantly influence by different riverine
inputs. Instead, IPDW weighs observations according to the shortest
water-path distance through the aquatic environment.

Model sensitivty was evaluated for distance power and max interpolation
range for each parameter. In IPDW, distance power determines how rapidly
a site's influence decays as path distance increases. The tested powers
(\(p\)) were 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25, 1.50, 2.00, 2.50, and 3.00.
The tested ranges were 5, 10, 15, and 20 km. All combinations were
evaluated for each parameter.

Spatial cross-validation was used to determine interpolation accuracy
using the blockCV package in R, which uses spatial folds to split data
into test and training data sets \citep{Valavi2019}. To assess
sensitivity to the spatial split, the fold-generation procedure was
repeated, each time refitting the IPDW model and comparing the results
to the held-out observation spatial-blocks. For this analysis 5 spatial
folds were used with 5 km spatial blocks, and blocks were assigned the
folds randomly. This process was then repeated across 5 random seeds in
which a new spatial partition was generated for each seed. For each
spatial fold, the monitoring locations were used in the training data to
construct the IPDW prediction surface. Withheld monitoring locations
were used to extract predicted values from the prediction raster and
compared to the observed station means.

Finally, model performance was evaluated using calculated
prediction-error statistics for each distance power - max range
combination. Each statistic was summarized across each repetition to
generate a mean absolute error (MAE), the standard deviation of the MAE,
the mean root mean squared error (RMSE) and its standard deviation, the
mean prediction bias (+/-) and finally the prediction coverage. Mean MAE
was the primary metric used in parameter selection, although in cases
where differences between several of the lowest results were negligible,
the smoother surfaces were given preference to better reflect the
reality of a well mixed natural system.

The final prediction surfaces were generated by fitting the IPDW
algorithm using the selected distance power and range on the full
dataset for each environmental variable. The selected settings were
evaluated using 5 km spatial blocks and five folds to calculate MAE,
RMSE, prediction bias, and prediction coverage.

\subsection{Species Distribution
Model}\label{species-distribution-model}

Oyster occurrence data had to go through some processing prior to being
used in the MaxEnt model. To avoid introducing unwanted spatial bias in
the model, the original 443 processed occurence locations were spatially
thinned to 1 km per point using the GeoThinneR package
\citep{GeoThinneR}. MaxEnt also requires the use of background data,
which are points who's locations characterize available environmental
conditions without being treated as oyster absences
\citep{Phillips2006, Elith2011, Phillips2009}. For this study, 10,000
background points were randomly generated in areas that had complete
prediction raster coverage, excluding any cells that already contained a
presence location.

The final environmental predictors used were dissolved oxygen, salinity,
sediment, turbidity, water temperature, and oyster recruitment. Sediment
was treated a a factor with codes 1, 2, and 3, representing mud, mudddy
sand, and sand; respectively.

The species distribution model was fit, tuned and evaluated using the
ENMeval package in r (\citet{Kass2021}). A presence-background MaxEnt
framework was used via the ``maxnet'' algorithm included in said
package. Final predictors used in model fitting were depth, dissolved
oxygen, salinity, sediment, turbidity, water temperature, and oyster
recruitment. Sediment was the only categorical variable. The prediction
domain was reduced to include areas that had a prediction cell value for
each of the prediction variables. To avoid introducing spatial bias into
the model, occurrence points were thinned to include only one point per
1km. 5,000 background points were randomly sampled from complete
prediction cells that did not have an occurrence point.

Spatial cross-validation was performed using ENMeval's included
four-partition block method. This method evenly divides occurrences by
northing, then easting, and finally assigns background points to each of
the blocks. Four model candidates were evaluated for performance with
increasing complexity using different combination of regularization
techniques: linear (L), quadratic (Q), hinge (H), and interactions (P) .
The four combinations used were L, LQ, LQH, and LQHP, with six
regularization multipliers each 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, and 4. Overall, this
resulted in 24 total model candidates and 96 fold evaluations.

Model validation was done using leave-one-out partitioning, repeating
until every partition had been withheld. Each model was evaluated by
calculating validation AUC against background points within the held-out
region, validation at 10\% training omission, AICc, and the number of
non-zero coefficients.

The final model was selected with the lowest mean 10th-percentile
omission, in the case of a tie the candidate model with the highest
Continuous Boyce Index (CBI) was chosen. Finally, the selected model was
fit to all oyster occurrence and background data points to generate the
final suitability raster. More information regarding model diagnositcs
in \textbf{?@tbl-eval-metrics}.

\subsubsection{HSI vs MaxEnt Comparison}\label{hsi-vs-maxent-comparison}

A modified habitat suitability index (HSI) was created using similar
methods presented in \citet{Geselbracht2024}. The data and methods used
were based on a PPBEP adaption of the original HSI. Original input data
was able to be obtained from PPBEP unfortunately the exact HSI model was
no longer available so it had to be recreated using their provided
methodology. The HSI was created using ArcGis Pro Suitability Modeler,
with the inptu rasters transformed into suitability scores and
subsequently combined using percentage weights. Environmental data used
included salinity, temperature, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll-a, and
turbidity, with only observations collected between May through October
in the years 2017 - 2023. It should be noted that this data was provided
as-is and as such, should not be considered equal to the previously
created environmental rasters in this study. The HSI also included the
same sediment and larval dispersal data used here. Finally, the model
included 2021 oyster distribution data, though it is not confirmed
whether it is the same dataset obatained earlier in this study. Each
variable was rated from 1 through 10, with all variables recieving a 1,
5 or 10. Where 1 = low, 5 = moderate, and 10 = high.
Table~\ref{tbl-hsi-classification-table} describes each classification
or transformation that was used. The HSI model output was then
normalized using the ArcGIS Pro Raster Calculator tool with the Min-Max
Normalization formula:

\[
\begin{gathered}
x'= \frac{ x - x_{min}}{x_{max}-x_{min}}\text{.} \\
\end{gathered} 
\]

Where \(0 \le x' \le 1\) and \$x \text{ is equal to any cell } i
\text{ at location } {[}j,k{]} \$.

The HSI was projected to align with the MaxEnt raster surface, grid
cells were resampled to 100m using bilinear resampling. A coverage mask
was created to ensure only cells that had values within both rasters
were used in the comparison. Finally, the rasters were classified using
equal intervals using breaks 0, 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, and 1.

Classified prediction raster agreement was described by determining the
count of cells of exact agreement, the count of cells in
psuedo-agreement (i.e.~those that fell within one class), and finally by
utilizing continuous Spearman correlation. Additionally, spatial
agreement was evaluated mathematically by subtracting the values of the
HSI from the values of the MaxEnt prediction surfaces, where;

\[ 
\begin{aligned}
\text{MaxEnt Favorability} : i_{jk} \le 0  \\
\text{Model Agreement} : i_{jk} = 0 \\
\text{HSI Favorability} : i_{jk} \ge 0 
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsection{Environmental Predictor
Results}\label{environmental-predictor-results}

Each parameter (save for chlorophyll-a) was interpolated into predicted
surface rasters using an inverse-path distance weighting algorithm. To
ensure the highest accuracy was achieved, a sensitivy analysis was
conducted on each parameter model using 36 distance-power/range
combinations, which were in turn evaluated across five random five-fold
spatial partitions. Powers ranged from 0.25 to 3.00, and maximum range
values evaluated were 5, 10, 15, and 20 km
(Figure~\ref{fig-ipdw-sensitivity}).

While the results of every sensitivity analysis will be reported here,
it should be noted that after running all parameters it was decided that
a distance power of 0.25 and a max range of 10km was to be used. This
decision was made as there was little variation in results of the
sensitivity analysis and a smoother prediction surface was preferred to
better represent the smoother gradients found in the real world.

\subsubsection{Dissolved Oxygen}\label{dissolved-oxygen}

Dissolved oxygen sensitivity had a mean MAE ranging from 0.426 to 0.507
mg/L. The lowest mean MAE with 100\% prediction was 0.425mg/L using a
distance power of 0.75 and max range of 10km
(Table~\ref{tbl-ipdw-validation}). The predicted rater surface values
ranged from 5.28mg/L to 7.99mg/L (n = 33,792; µ = 6.52; σ = 0.26;
\textbf{?@tbl-raster-summary}). The final prediction model achieved
100\% coverage with a MAE of 0.431 mg/L (σ = 0.006) with RMSE = 0.552
mg/L (σ = 0.004). The model tended to over predict by +0.013 mg/L.
Spatially, dissolved oxygen generally decreased in the western portions
of the study area. With the lowest values appearing in East Bay and
northern Blackwater Bay (Figure~\ref{fig-environmental-rasters}).

\subsubsection{Salinity}\label{salinity}

The salinity sensitivity analysis had a mean MAE ranging from 2.397 ppt
to 3.125 ppt. The lowest reported MAE with 100\% model coverage was 2.49
ppt using distance power 0.50 and max range of 10km. The absolute lowest
mean MAE was 2.397 ppt was achieved using distance power of 3.00 and a
max range of 5km , but model coverage fell to 96.1\%. The difference in
mean MAE between the lowest achieved DP-MR combination and the actual
DP-MR combination used was 0.000119 ppt
(Table~\ref{tbl-ipdw-validation}). Spatial cross-validation of the final
model fit had a MAE of 2.49 ppt (σ = 0.036) and RMSE 3.444 ppt (σ =
0.024). The model slightly over predicted salinity values (+0.140 ppt)
and achieved 100\% coverage. The predicted values ranged from 2.31 to
26.73 ppt (n = 33,792; µ = 16.36 ppt; σ = 3.14;
\textbf{?@tbl-raster-summary}). Spatially, salinity generally decreased
from west to east with the lowest values being in the northern
Blackwater Bay and the highest values located in the southwestern region
of Pensacola Bay (Figure~\ref{fig-environmental-rasters}).

\subsubsection{Turbidity}\label{turbidity}

The sensitivity analysis for turbidity reported a mean MAE range from
0.696 to 0.847 NTU. The lowest mean MAE was 0.696 NTU (DP = 1.00, MR =
15km) with 95.1\% model coverage (Table~\ref{tbl-ipdw-validation}). The
final model fit produced a MAE = 0.745 NTU (σ = 0.018) with RMSE = 0.937
NTU (σ = 0.026). Overall the model slightly under predicted by -0.010
NTU and achieved 95.1\% coverage. The final predicted surface values
ranged from 0.03 NTU to 5.23 NTU (n = 33,790; µ = 2.23 NTU; σ = 0.47;
\textbf{?@tbl-raster-summary}). Spatially, turbidity generally decreased
from north to south, with the highest values occuring near the mouth of
Escambia River and the lowest values occuring in the southwestern
portion of Pensacola Bay (Figure~\ref{fig-environmental-rasters}).

\subsubsection{Water Temperature}\label{water-temperature}

Mean MAE for the water temperature sensitivity analysis ranged from
0.978 to 1.135 degrees C. The lowest mean MAE was 0.978 degrees C with
100\% model coverage (DP = 0.75, MR = 10km;
Table~\ref{tbl-ipdw-validation}). The final model had a MAE of 0.988
degrees C (σ = 0.015) and RMSE was 1.163 degrees C (σ = 0.014). The
model generally under predicted water temperature by -0.063 degrees C
with 100\% model coverage. The resulting prediction surface ranged from
25.93 to 30.43 degrees C (n = 33,792; µ = 27.66°C; σ = 0.41°C;
\textbf{?@tbl-raster-summary}). Water temperature was generally lower in
the eastern portions of the study area, with the coolest values trending
toward Blackwater Bay (Figure~\ref{fig-environmental-rasters}).
